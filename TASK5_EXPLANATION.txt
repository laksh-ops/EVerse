3. WHAT ARE AVERAGE SPECTRAL FEATURES?
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
A Short-Time Fourier Transform (STFT) produces a 2D time-frequency matrix:
    S(f, t) of dimensions: (Number of Frequency Bins) x (Number of Time Frames)

Problem:
A reference song is typically 2 to 4 minutes long (thousands of time frames),
while a query clip is only 5 to 10 seconds long (hundreds of time frames).
Because their lengths differ and the query's start time in the song is unknown,
we cannot directly compute a frame-by-frame matrix difference.

Solution:
We collapse the time dimension by computing the time-average of the decibel
magnitude across all time frames:
    S_mean(f) = (1 / T) * sum_{t=1}^{T} S(f, t)

Optionally, to capture frequency dynamics and variance across the clip, we can
also compute the standard deviation across time frames:
    S_std(f) = sqrt( (1 / T) * sum_{t=1}^{T} (S(f, t) - S_mean(f))^2 )

Feature Vector:
We restrict frequencies to the musically active spectrum (0 Hz to 5000 Hz) to
eliminate inaudible high-frequency noise. The resulting 1D vector S_mean(f) acts
as a static "spectral footprint" or timbral summary of the audio track.


4. SIMILARITY MEASURES & MATHEMATICAL FORMULATIONS
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
Let u be the 1D feature vector of the query clip.
Let v be the 1D feature vector of a candidate song in the database.

A. Cosine Similarity (Primary & Recommended Metric):
   Formula:
       Cosine(u, v) = (u . v) / ( ||u||_2 * ||v||_2 )
                    = sum_i (u_i * v_i) / ( sqrt(sum_i u_i^2) * sqrt(sum_i v_i^2) )

   Why it is preferred for audio:
   - Cosine similarity measures the angle between the two vectors in high-dimensional
     frequency space.
   - It is strictly scale/volume invariant: if the query clip is recorded at a lower
     or higher volume, the vector is simply multiplied by a scalar c > 0 (c * u).
     Because the norm in the denominator scales proportionally, Cosine(c * u, v) = Cosine(u, v).
   - Values range from -1.0 to +1.0 (with +1.0 meaning identical spectral profile).

B. Pearson Correlation Coefficient:
   Formula:
       r(u, v) = sum_i ( (u_i - mean(u)) * (v_i - mean(v)) ) /
                 ( sqrt(sum_i (u_i - mean(u))^2) * sqrt(sum_i (v_i - mean(v))^2) )

   - Mean-centers both feature vectors before computing cosine similarity.
   - Evaluates whether spectral peaks and valleys rise and fall together across
     the frequency bands.

C. Normalized Euclidean Similarity:
   Formula:
       Dist(u, v) = ||u - v||_2 = sqrt( sum_i (u_i - v_i)^2 )
       Sim_euc(u, v) = 1 / ( 1 + Dist(u, v) )

   - Measures absolute point-to-point geometric distance.
   - More sensitive to overall energy level shifts than Cosine similarity.


5. RECOGNITION DECISION RULE
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
1. Extract query feature vector: u = extract_average_spectral_features(query_audio)
2. For each song S_k in the database (k = 1, 2, 3):
       score_k = compute_similarity(u, v_k)
3. Prediction:
       Best Song = argmax_{S_k} (score_k)
       Best Score = max (score_k)


6. ADVANCED REFINEMENT: SLIDING-WINDOW (CHUNKED) MATCHING
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
Limitation of Global Averaging:
A full song changes over time (intro, verse, chorus, bridge, outro). Averaging
the entire 3-minute song dilutes distinctive section-specific frequency content.

Sliding-Window Solution:
- Instead of comparing the query against the full song's average, divide the
  reference song into sliding chunks matching the query duration (e.g., 10 seconds).
- Compute the similarity between the query and each chunk across the entire song.
- The candidate song's score is taken as the maximum similarity across all chunks:
      Score(Song_k) = max_{chunk in Song_k} Cosine(u_query, chunk_feature)
- The chunk with the highest score also yields an accurate estimate of the query's
  starting timestamp in the song!


7. WHY SIMPLE SPECTRAL MATCHING WORKS (AND WHY SHAZAM USES TASK 6)
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
Comparison Table:

| Criterion              | Simple Spectral Matching (Task 5)     | Peak-Pair Hashing (Task 6 / Shazam)       |
|------------------------|---------------------------------------|-------------------------------------------|
| Temporal Order         | Lost (averaging removes all timing)   | Preserved (pairs relative time delta_t)   |
| Scrambled Audio Notes  | Cannot distinguish scrambled audio    | Rejects scrambled audio immediately       |
| Noise Robustness       | Low (noise shifts the spectral mean)  | High (noise adds isolated random peaks)   |
| Database Scalability   | O(N) linear search over dense vectors | O(1) inverted hash table lookup           |
| Computational Cost     | Minimal (single STFT + vector dot)    | Moderate (peak finding + hash indexing)   |
| Best Use Case          | Genre/timbre classification, baseline | Exact audio identification from audio mic |

Conclusion:
Task 5 provides an essential, lightning-fast baseline recogniser that works well
when reference songs have distinctly different frequency signatures (e.g. bass-heavy
vs acoustic vs synth-dominant). However, its fundamental limitation—the loss of time
information—demonstrates why Task 6 (peak-pair hashing with time offset voting) is
the definitive solution for Shazam-like music identification.
